The best automation opportunity is not always the most impressive workflow. It is often the repetitive coordination that prevents a capable team from spending time on more valuable work.

01

Look for repeated coordination

Document the handoffs, copying, reminders, classification, and status checks that happen every day. These tasks often consume more collective attention than teams realise.

Map the trigger, the information involved, the decision, and the desired output before discussing tools.

02

Separate rules from judgement

Stable, repeatable rules are strong automation candidates. Ambiguous decisions, sensitive communication, and exceptions may need a person to remain responsible.

A useful workflow makes that boundary explicit rather than treating human oversight as an afterthought.

03

Prototype the smallest complete workflow

Test one real route from trigger to outcome. Include error states, missing information, ownership, and a visible record of what happened.

A narrow prototype exposes operational reality faster than a broad demonstration built around ideal data.

04

Measure time and quality together

Time saved matters, but so do accuracy, visibility, response speed, and the effort required to maintain the workflow.

The goal is useful space for better work, not automation volume for its own sake.

“Automate the repeatable coordination; keep responsibility and judgement clearly human.”

Key takeaways

Keep these four points.

  • Start with repetitive coordination, not a tool.
  • Keep judgement and accountability visible.
  • Prototype one complete workflow with exceptions.
  • Measure quality alongside time saved.
